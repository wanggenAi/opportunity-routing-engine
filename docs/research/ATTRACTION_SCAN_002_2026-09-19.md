## Retained formation — ATTRACTION_SCAN_002-F1

### Working title

**Residential Private-Charger Permission Pool**

### A-side actor

Private EV charger / parking-space holders with idle charging windows.

### A-side temptation

Allow safe use during idle periods and receive service income without losing personal charging priority or manually coordinating each user.

### A-side evidence

A Shanghai Jiading community pilot reported:
- 225 private chargers;
- around 70% daytime vacancy;
- residents without long-term fixed spaces unable to install their own chargers;
- community-organized sharing through app authorization / sharing arrangements.

Independent policy and commercial evidence also shows the private-charger-sharing model is actively being implemented.

Sources:
- https://www.shobserver.com/sgh/detail?id=1743039
- https://news.10jqka.com.cn/20260430/c676416896.shtml

Truth state: `CURRENT BEHAVIOR / PILOT EVIDENCE`.

### B-side actor

Residential EV owners who lack an installable fixed charger or face scarce public/community charging capacity.

### B-side temptation

Gain nearby, predictable, lower-friction charging without competing for a small public charging pool or traveling to a distant commercial station.

### B-side evidence

Current Suzhou community reporting describes:
- “车多桩少”;
- prior public-charger competition;
- resident calls for non-competitive shared charging;
- old-community power-capacity and fixed-space constraints.

Source:
- https://www.sipac.gov.cn/szgyyq/mtjj/202609/77441a3b6288472c9784b543bde1e688.shtml

Truth state: `CURRENT BEHAVIOR / RECURRING NEED`.

### Permission actor

Property company / community / parking manager / owners' governance body.

This third actor is not optional.

It controls or materially influences:
- vehicle entry;
- shared-area use;
- parking order;
- fire/safety rules;
- electrical infrastructure coordination;
- common-property commercial use;
- revenue allocation.

### Permission actor temptation

Reduce charging complaints and capex pressure while retaining control over access, safety, parking order and possible revenue sharing.

Policy evidence:
- National Energy Administration has encouraged property-company participation in charging construction/operation, cost-sharing and revenue-sharing mechanisms, and “统建统服” residential charging models.
- Current judicial guidance distinguishes a resident's right to install a charger on a fixed-use space from broader common-area governance, while encouraging concentrated planning where appropriate.

Sources:
- https://www.nea.gov.cn/20250729/01c60c063f824da1a7e993fdfef8668a/c.html
- https://legalinfo.moj.gov.cn/zxxfyasf/202607/t20260720_537627.html

Truth state: `INSTITUTIONAL INCENTIVE + CONTROL`.

## Structural friction hypothesis

The constraint is not primarily “there is no charger.”

It is:

```
PRIVATE CAPACITY
+
NEARBY DEMAND
+
NO SAFE / LEGIBLE PERMISSION TO CROSS THE PROPERTY BOUNDARY
```

For a private charger to become a shared community resource, the transaction may need simultaneous control over:

- allowed user;
- allowed time window;
- parking-space availability;
- community / gate access;
- charger authorization;
- electricity measurement;
- service fee;
- owner priority;
- overtime / non-move penalties;
- property share;
- fault handling;
- safety rules;
- settlement record.

The hypothesis is that the missing edge is a **permissioned resource pool**, not a marketplace listing.

Truth state: `EVIDENCED FRICTION / EDGE NOT YET PROVEN`.

## Connection pressure

### A-side pressure

Idle equipment/time plus potential service income.

### B-side pressure

Repeated charging requirement plus inability to install or reliably access nearby charging.

### Permission-side pressure

Resident complaints, electrical capacity constraints, parking conflicts and need to govern shared access.

Result: `MULTILATERAL PRESSURE EVIDENCED`.

## Attraction Brief

### A-SIDE TEMPTATION

“My charger and parking access sit idle for long periods; I would share if my own use remains first priority, strangers cannot create parking/safety problems, and settlement is automatic.”

### B-SIDE TEMPTATION

“I need nearby charging repeatedly but cannot install a private charger or reliably get a public charger; I would use a controlled shared slot if access and availability are dependable.”

### PERMISSION-SIDE TEMPTATION

“I need to reduce charging conflict without uncontrolled outside vehicles or unmanaged private transactions; I may accept sharing if rules, identity, access, safety and settlement are enforceable.”

### OPERATOR TEMPTATION

High at the structural level because the work is resource activation through permission, scheduling and settlement rather than recurring physical delivery.

But operator attraction is conditional on proving that property adoption can be routed through repeatable channels rather than founder-by-founder community negotiation.

### CONNECTION WOW

The value jump is not another charger.

It is changing an already-installed private asset from:

`CLOSED / OWNER-ONLY / IDLE`

to

`PERMISSIONED / SCHEDULED / SHARED / SETTLED`

without removing owner priority or property control.

### ACTIVATION FRICTION

High and decisive:
- property participation;
- owners' governance / common-area rules;
- parking-space rights;
- gate integration;
- electricity capacity;
- charger compatibility;
- hardware retrofit;
- safety / insurance;
- tax / settlement;
- resident objections;
- local operations.

### SELF-PROPULSION

Plausible but unproven.

A successful property/community deployment could create:
- reusable property rules;
- standard access templates;
- charger compatibility knowledge;
- owner conversion playbooks;
- utilization data;
- predictable demand clusters;
- group-level rollout across multiple properties.

If every community needs bespoke political/social negotiation and hardware redesign, self-propulsion fails.

## Operator non-labor test

The formation fails if the operator must personally:
- canvass individual charger owners;
- persuade residents one-by-one;
- negotiate every property;
- mediate parking disputes;
- install/repair hardware;
- manually settle payments;
- manage daily charging incidents.

It can survive only if acquisition and permission can be concentrated through actors such as:
- property groups;
- parking operators;
- charger operators;
- community energy-service providers;
- standardized local implementation partners.

Current result: `UNPROVEN`.

## Decisive next questions

1. What exactly prevents an existing private-charger platform from entering a willing community today?
2. Is the bottleneck property sales, resident consent, electrical capacity, hardware compatibility, legal allocation, insurance, or unit economics?
3. Can permission be granted at property-group / parking-operator level, or only community by community?
4. Which charger brands can be shared by software authorization without hardware replacement?
5. What retrofit cost is required for meter / lock / gate / network integration?
6. Who legally receives electricity/service revenue and who bears fault/liability?
7. Does property revenue materially improve willingness to participate?
8. Are community usage densities high enough for meaningful owner/property economics?
9. How often does a shared private charger actually transact after activation?
10. Do existing operators already solve the governance layer, making the remaining task ordinary local channel sales?

Until those are answered:
